\section{Choosing a resolution}\label{sec:resolution}
A program never assumes a screen. The panel decides what the machine
shows --- 1920$\times$1080, 1366$\times$768, whatever the computer has --- and
K/OS picks one of that panel's integer display resolutions for itself. A
program does one of two things:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item \textbf{Use the screen it is given.} VICKY says its size
  (\texttt{\$D0D6} GLASSW, \texttt{\$D0D8} GLASSH; Pascal's
  \verb|GetMaxX| and \verb|GetMaxY| after \verb|InitGraph(0)|), and the
  program places things as shares of it.
\item \textbf{Ask for a size.} \verb|MODE 640x480| --- from the prompt, from
  a program through the shell (\verb|rom_shell| in C, \verb|SetResolution(640, 480, false)| in Pascal's \verb|k4510| unit, \verb|*MODE 640x480| in
  BASIC). If the panel has an integer resolution of that size, that is
  it. If not, it is \textbf{Integer Best Fit}: exactly 640$\times$480
  pixels, shown at the largest whole multiple the panel holds, centred,
  the rest bordered in the frame colour --- on 1920$\times$1080, twice,
  1280$\times$960 with borders of 320 and 60. Nothing is blurred.
  K/OS lays its text out on it (80$\times$30 in 8$\times$16 cells), and when
  the program ends the shell's own screen comes back.
\end{itemize}

\begin{sloppypar} % code words this long leave the first line no break
\noindent \textbf{Effects are asked for, never given.}
\texttt{MODE 640x480 -{}-smooth} (\verb|-m|; \texttt{SetResolution(640, 480, true)})
fills the panel instead, by the largest whole multiple and then smoothing
--- sharp-bilinear, soft only at the last step. \verb|MODE -c|
(\verb|--scanlines|) lays scanlines over whatever mode is up: the lower
half of every machine row dimmed, as a CRT's beam left a dark gap between
lines --- only where a row is two panel lines or more, so not at scale~1.
A new size keeps them; \verb|MODE -p| (\verb|--plain|) takes every effect
off. The two combine: \verb|MODE 400x300 -m -c|. A size larger than the
panel is refused: no whole multiple fits, and shrinking is not a fit.
\verb|RES.BAS| in \pth{/LANG/BASIC/EX} tours all of it, drawing on each
screen with \verb|GRAPHICS 3|.
Underneath it is VICKY's software resolution: \texttt{\$D0D0} GLASSCTL~2,
the size in \texttt{\$D0D2}-\texttt{\$D0D5}, bit~4 for smoothing, bit~6
for scanlines (Appendix~\ref{cha:registers}).
\end{sloppypar}

\section{A split screen: SHEILA}\label{sec:split}
The Apple~II put four lines of text under its graphics; C64 programmers
did the same with a raster interrupt timed to the line. On this machine
the CPU does nothing at all: SHEILA, VICKY's display-list coprocessor
(the Amiga's copper is its ancestor), runs a short list from memory at
every frame, in step with the raster, and a register it writes takes
effect on the line about to be drawn. A split is six instructions:

\begin{type}
MOVE $10,$00     line 0: layer 0, the console's text, off
MOVE $20,$19     layer 1, a 640-wide 8 bpp bitmap, on
WAIT 416         26 text rows of 16 lines: the split
MOVE $20,$00     the bitmap off
MOVE $10,$07     the text on: rows 26-29 are text
END              and again from the top, next frame
\end{type}

\noindent With the status bands up, the split has three parts rather
than two: the text layer stays on at line~0 for the top band, the
bitmap takes over at the band's end, and the text comes back for the
last four rows and the bottom band --- eight instructions. \verb|SPLIT|
reads where those lines fall from the console itself (\texttt{\$DA06},
its rows, and \texttt{\$DA08}, the rows above it), so it is right with
the bands up or down. Each instruction is four bytes --- the operation (\texttt{1}
WAIT, \texttt{2} MOVE, \texttt{0} END), then its operands --- and the
list is anywhere in memory, a 28-bit address in \texttt{\$D060}--\texttt{\$D063},
started by \texttt{1} in \texttt{\$D064}. A MOVE is exactly a write by
the CPU, so any VICKY register can change mid-frame: the mode, the
scroll, a layer, the palette. Two rules: line numbers count the glass,
0--479, whatever the mode (in a 240-line mode each line is drawn twice,
so split on an even one); and \textbf{nothing turns SHEILA off after a
program}, so a program that turns it on turns it off when it leaves.

\screen{shots/split.png}{\texttt{SPLIT}: blitter lines above, the
console's last four rows below, and SHEILA holding the line between
them.}

\verb|SPLIT| is the demonstration, and it has a twin that draws the
same ribbon of lines --- endpoints bouncing, the line sixteen behind
erased --- as fast as it can, and says how fast in the text band:
\pth{/LANG/BASIC/EX/SPLIT.BAS}, with K4510 BASIC's own
\verb|LINE|. At the 40.5\,MHz a host starts at:

\noindent The blitter draws a line in no time at all, so the numbers
are the languages: what a BASIC spends is its own arithmetic, its
arrays and its line lookups, and three hundred lines a second is a
good deal more than a game of 1985 asked for. The split itself costs
every one of them nothing. A faster clock (F12, or \verb|SETUP|) scales
all three.
